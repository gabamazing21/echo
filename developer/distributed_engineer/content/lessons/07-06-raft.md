# Consensus: the Raft algorithm

> **Step 7 · Distributed systems theory · Week 3**
> Concept: *leader election, log replication, safety*

This is the summit. **Consensus** is getting a group of nodes to **agree on a
value** (or a sequence of values) even though nodes crash and the network drops,
delays, and reorders messages. **Raft** is a consensus algorithm designed to be
*understandable* — and you'll implement it yourself in Step 8.

## Why this matters

Consensus is the bedrock of reliable distributed systems. It's how you get exactly
one leader (no split brain), a replicated log everyone agrees on, distributed locks,
and consistent configuration. etcd (which Kubernetes runs on), Consul, and
CockroachDB all run Raft. Implementing it is the single most convincing line on a
distributed-engineer résumé. **Read the Raft paper twice** — this lesson is your
guide through it.

## 1. The problem consensus solves

Recall from Step 6: a single-row write conflict is "two parties want to set the same
state; agree on the result without losing writes." Consensus is that problem across
*many machines* under *unreliable networks*. Formally, consensus must guarantee:

- **Agreement** — all non-faulty nodes decide the same value.
- **Validity** — the decided value was actually proposed by someone.
- **Termination** — non-faulty nodes eventually decide.

Raft achieves this for a **replicated log**: every node ends up with the same
sequence of commands in the same order, so applying them yields identical state on
every node (a *replicated state machine*).

## 2. The big idea: one leader, one log

Raft simplifies consensus by electing a **single leader** that owns the log. All
changes flow through the leader, which replicates them to **followers**. If the
leader fails, the cluster elects a new one. Reducing consensus to "elect a leader,
then have it impose order" is what makes Raft understandable. Three roles:

- **Leader** — handles all client requests, replicates log entries.
- **Follower** — passive; responds to leader/candidate RPCs.
- **Candidate** — a follower trying to become leader during an election.

## 3. Terms — logical time

Raft divides time into **terms**, numbered consecutively. Each term has *at most one*
leader and begins with an election. Terms are Raft's **logical clock** — they solve
the "you can't trust wall-clocks" problem (lesson 1). Every message carries a term;
a node seeing a *higher* term immediately updates and steps down to follower. Stale
leaders (from an old term) are thus harmless — their messages are rejected.

## 4. Leader election (Lab 2A in Step 8)

- Followers expect periodic **heartbeats** (empty AppendEntries) from the leader.
- If a follower hears nothing for a randomized **election timeout**, it becomes a
  **candidate**, increments the term, votes for itself, and sends **RequestVote**
  RPCs to all peers.
- A node grants its vote if it hasn't voted this term *and* the candidate's log is
  at least as up-to-date as its own.
- Win a **majority** of votes → become leader → start sending heartbeats.
- **Split vote?** No one gets a majority; the term ends with no leader. Randomized
  timeouts make simultaneous candidacies unlikely, so a later election succeeds.

> **Why majority (quorum)?** Any two majorities of the same cluster overlap in at
> least one node. That overlap is what prevents two leaders in the same term and
> guarantees a new leader's log contains every committed entry. Majority quorums are
> the mathematical heart of Raft. (This is why Raft clusters are odd-sized: 3, 5, 7.)

## 5. Log replication (Lab 2B)

- A client command goes to the leader, which **appends** it to its log (uncommitted).
- The leader sends **AppendEntries** RPCs to replicate the entry to followers.
- Once a **majority** have stored the entry, the leader marks it **committed**,
  applies it to its state machine, and returns to the client. It tells followers the
  commit index so they apply it too.
- **Consistency check:** each AppendEntries includes the index+term of the preceding
  entry. A follower rejects it if its log doesn't match there; the leader then walks
  *back* until the logs align and overwrites the follower's divergent tail. This
  forces all logs to converge to the leader's.

## 6. Safety — the property that makes it correct

Election + replication aren't enough; Raft adds rules so a committed entry is **never
lost**, even across leader changes:

- **Election restriction**: a candidate can only win if its log is at least as
  up-to-date as a majority's — so a node missing committed entries can't become
  leader.
- **Leader append-only**: a leader never overwrites or deletes its own entries.
- **Commitment rule**: a leader only commits entries from its *current* term by
  counting replicas (older-term entries get committed indirectly, once a current-
  term entry on top is committed) — closing a subtle hole where a committed entry
  could otherwise be overwritten.

Together these give the **State Machine Safety** property: if any node has applied a
log entry at a given index, no other node ever applies a *different* entry at that
index. That's what "agreement" means in practice.

## 7. Persistence & snapshots (Lab 2C / 2D)

- Each node must persist `currentTerm`, `votedFor`, and its log **before responding**
  to RPCs, so a crash-and-restart doesn't violate safety.
- The log grows forever; **snapshots** compact it by saving the state machine's state
  and discarding entries before the snapshot point.

## Common interview gotchas

- **4 nodes tolerate the same 1 failure as 3 — that's why you go odd.** Majority is `floor(N/2)+1`, so 3→2 and 4→3; fault tolerance is `floor(N/2)`: both 3 and 4 survive only 1 failure. Each extra tolerated failure costs *two* nodes (5 tolerates 2). Worse, an even cluster can split exactly in half and stall with no majority either side.
- **"Replicated to a majority" is NOT "committed."** Figure 8 (§5.4.2): a leader must *not* commit a prior-term entry just because replica-count hit a majority — a higher-term log can still overwrite it. A leader may replica-count only entries from its **current term**; old-term entries commit *indirectly*, once a current-term entry above them commits.
- **A partitioned old leader keeps taking writes but can never commit them.** Stuck in the minority, it appends client commands forever yet never reaches a majority, so nothing acks. On heal it sees the new leader's higher term, steps down, and its uncommitted tail is discarded — no data loss, because none of it was ever committed.
- **Raft is CP: lose quorum, lose writes.** If a majority isn't alive and reachable, no leader can be elected and nothing commits — the cluster freezes writes rather than risk split-brain. It chooses consistency over availability by design.
- **Randomized election timeouts, not synchronized clocks, prevent split votes.** Raft never trusts wall-clocks for correctness; staggering timeouts just makes simultaneous candidacies unlikely so an election converges quickly.
